Before I set up a workbench on the far shore, I worked inside the giants.

I built design systems for national healthcare companies, the kind with a thousand screens and a legal department that reviews the color of a button. I sat in agency war rooms at two in the morning, the good kind of tired, watching a brand come together out of nothing but argument and coffee. I made interfaces that millions of people used without ever knowing my name, which is most of the work, honestly. Nobody thanks the person who moved the checkout button an inch to the left and quietly saved them from abandoning their cart.

People assume the lesson of the corporate years is the polish. It isn't. The polish is real, and I'm grateful for it, but polish is the easy thing to carry home. What I actually took was three things I could not have learned in a small town.

The first is how a big system holds together, or fails to. When you have a thousand screens, you learn fast that the enemy is inconsistency. Every one-off decision is a small debt, and the debts compound until the whole thing groans under its own weight. The giants taught me to build the rules first and the pages second. A small organization thinks it is too small to need that discipline. It is exactly backwards. The smaller you are, the less you can afford the debt.

The second is that speed is a moral issue. At the scale of millions, a page that loads half a second slower costs real money and real patience, so nobody is allowed to pretend it does not matter. I watched teams fight for that half-second like it was the last lifeboat. Then I came home and found small websites carrying forty tracking scripts and a five-second load, built by people who had been told that is just how the web is now. It is not how the web is now. It is how the web is when nobody's job depends on it being otherwise.

The third thing is quieter, and it is the one that sent me home. The giants can do almost anything, and they will do almost nothing that does not scale to a million users. There is a whole territory of good work, the specific, the local, the human-sized, that a big company simply cannot see, because it is not worth their while. A nonprofit bleeding donors through a broken form. A gallery that needs to sell a print without handing thirty percent to a platform. A shop with an inventory quirk no template will ever accommodate. That work is invisible from inside the castle. It is the only work I can see clearly now.

So I left. I kept the discipline, the respect for speed, the habit of building rules before pages. I left behind the meetings about meetings, the eleven layers of approval, the quiet agreement that nothing made for fewer than a million people is worth making well.

Turns out it is worth making well. Sometimes it is the only thing that is.
