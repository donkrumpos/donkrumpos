I do my thinking on walks, mostly along the lake, and lately I have been noticing that the natural world is embarrassingly good at the exact problem the software industry pretends is hard.

Consider a forest. Underneath it, threaded through the soil, is a network of fungus connecting the trees, root to root, passing sugar and water and even warnings from one organism to the next. Ecologists have taken to calling it the wood wide web, which is a little cute, but the comparison runs deeper than the pun. It is a real network. It routes resources. It is resilient, because there is no central server in the middle of the forest that everything depends on. Cut any single thread and the traffic finds another way. No company owns it. No one pays a monthly fee to stay connected. It simply grows, and mends itself, and persists.

A watershed does the same trick with water. A marsh does it with life. None of these systems were designed by a committee, and not one of them is rented.

Now consider how we have decided to build things on the computer. We put everything on somebody else's server, in somebody else's building, under somebody else's terms. We pay rent to stay connected to our own information. We build systems with a single point of failure and a monthly invoice, and then we act surprised when the platform goes down, or gets sold, or triples the price, and takes our small corner of the world with it.

The forest would find this baffling. All that fragility, on purpose, for a fee.

I am not going to pretend a website is a woodland. But the principles that keep a forest standing are the exact ones I try to build by, and they are the opposite of how the industry currently works. Own your ground. Depend on as little as possible that you do not control. Build it so that if any one piece fails, the rest keeps standing. Make it cheap to maintain and slow to rot. Let it be a thing that persists on its own, quietly, without a subscription keeping it alive like a machine in a hospital.

There is an old idea in the esoteric traditions, as above, so below, the notion that the same patterns repeat at every scale of the world. I make no claims about the cosmos. But I know that a good system, whether it is a marsh or a donor database, tends to look the same when it is built right. Distributed. Resilient. Owned by the people who live in it. Beautiful, in a way that turns out to be indistinguishable from working well.

This is the direction I am building in, and it is why I came home to build it. Not because the small town has better internet, it does not, but because you cannot learn this from inside a company that sells subscriptions. You learn it on foot, by the water, watching a system that has been debugging itself for four hundred million years and has never once sent anyone an invoice.

Come along, if you like. I will be showing the work as it comes off the bench.
