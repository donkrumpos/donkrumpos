There is a sentence by William Morris I keep taped, more or less, to the inside of my skull: have nothing in your houses that you do not know to be useful, or believe to be beautiful.

Morris was a designer, a poet, and a magnificent crank, and in the second half of the eighteen hundreds he watched the machine age flood England with cheap, ugly, disposable stuff. Furniture stamped out by the thousand. Wallpaper printed with poison. Objects made as fast and as cheap as possible by people who would never own them, for people who would never meet the maker. He and a loose band of designers, weavers, printers and troublemakers said no. Make things by hand. Make them well. Make them useful and beautiful at once. Let the person who uses a thing know the person who made it, and let both of them own it outright.

They called it Arts and Crafts, and if you have ever run your hand along a well-joined oak table and felt the difference, you already understand the whole argument.

Here is what took me embarrassingly long to notice. We are living through the same moment again, and this time the cheap stamped-out thing is software.

Look at how most small organizations get a website now. They rent a template that ten thousand other organizations also rent. They fill it with their content and their hopes, and they pay every month, forever, for the privilege. If they stop paying, it vanishes. They do not own the thing. They cannot take it with them. They cannot open the hood. When the platform decides to change the rules, or raise the rent, or retire the one feature they depend on, they find out by email, the way a tenant finds out the building has been sold.

This is the machine age again. Mass-produced, disposable, identical from one town to the next, made by people who will never meet you, for a fee that never ends. And just like the first time, everyone has been told this is simply how it is now. Progress. Convenience. Be grateful.

Morris and his crowd lost the argument in the short run. The machines were cheaper, and the century wanted cheap. But they were right, and the proof is that a hundred and fifty years later we still pay more for the handmade table, the letterpress card, the loaf from the actual baker. Not out of nostalgia. Because the made-with-care version is genuinely better, and some stubborn part of us refuses to forget it.

I build websites the Arts and Crafts way. By hand, to fit, to last, and yours to keep. No monthly rent on your own front door. No template you share with ten thousand strangers. The same engineering the big companies use, joined together carefully for one organization and handed over so completely that you could, in theory, never speak to me again.

Morris would have hated computers. I think he would have loved this. It is the same fight he was already having, only moved indoors, onto the glowing rectangle, where the machine age went to hide.
